\begin{abstract}
Bilinear multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) replace element-wise nonlinearities with the
element-wise product of two linear maps, which makes the layer's computation an exact
quadratic form in its input. \citet{pearce2025bilinear} showed that eigendecomposing
the resulting interaction matrices, using the weights alone, recovers interpretable
features in image classifiers and traceable circuits in language models. We first
reproduce this work across both domains, organizing it into seven independently
testable claims; six reproduce, and the seventh reproduces up to caveats we trace to
a specific obstacle, the unavailability of the cleaned dataset behind the published
checkpoints. We then extend weight-based interpretability to variational
autoencoders, where no output has a label. On the encoder side the method transfers
directly and its features are causal: synthetic inputs built from eigenvectors alone
are correctly recognized by the encoder in 9--10 of 10 classes across three
datasets, far above random-initialization baselines. On the decoder side a naive
transfer fails, and we show why: the interaction matrix is linear in the probe
direction, so overlapping class-mean targets manufacture a spurious near-universal
generative direction. Centering the targets is the canonical correction, and with it
weight-based generation succeeds in 9 to 9.5 of 10 classes, robustly across latent
dimensions 10 to 32. We further prove that a purely bilinear decoder is an exactly
even function of its latent code, identify the one-line architectural fix, and
report two carefully controlled negative results: gradient-based baselines on an
ordinary VAE match the weight-based method's raw capability, locating its value in
data-free, globally valid closed-form structure rather than synthesis power; and a
hypothesized encoder--decoder eigenvector alignment is null even when an explicit
alignment loss demonstrably optimizes its own objective during training. Code,
per-experiment results, and trained checkpoints are available in our repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most interpretability methods explain a network through its activations on data.
Saliency maps attribute a single prediction to input pixels \citep{simonyan2014deep},
and sparse dictionary learning decomposes hidden activations over a dataset into more
interpretable atoms \citep{bricken2023monosemanticity, cunningham2024sparse}. Both
families describe what a model does on the inputs we happen to feed it. They say
little about the function stored in the weights, and their conclusions may not hold
off-distribution.

\citet{pearce2025bilinear} take the opposite route. Building on a technical note by
\citet{sharkey2023technical}, they study bilinear MLPs, a variant of the Gated Linear
Unit \citep{shazeer2020glu} without the gate nonlinearity,
\begin{equation}
  g(\vx) = (\mW\vx) \odot (\mV\vx),
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ is the element-wise product. Because no nonlinearity intervenes, the
activation of any output direction is an exact quadratic form $\vx^\top \mQ \vx$ whose
interaction matrix $\mQ$ is built from the weights alone. Eigendecomposing $\mQ$ then
yields orthogonal input directions with real-valued importances, computable without a
single forward pass. The paper demonstrates this on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST
classifiers, and extends it to transformers with bilinear MLP blocks, where
interaction matrices connect sparse autoencoder (SAE) features across a layer and
expose a sentiment-negation circuit directly in the weights.

This paper makes two contributions. The first is an independent reproduction of that
work. We reproduce the main figures and appendices of \citet{pearce2025bilinear} in
both the vision and the language settings, quantify results that the original
presents qualitatively, and record every deviation we encountered together with its
cause. Reproducing the language experiments required more than rerunning released
code: the research repository behind the paper is no longer public, the cleaned
training dataset was never released, and the vendored library predates breaking
changes in its dependencies. Part of our contribution is documenting how far the
published artifacts alone support the paper's claims, and where the missing pieces
matter.

The second contribution extends weight-based interpretability to a setting the
original paper does not consider: generative models trained without labels.
Classifiers hand the analyst a meaningful output direction for free, one per class.
A variational autoencoder \citep{kingma2014auto} does not, and its two halves pose
different versions of the problem: the encoder's interaction matrices live in pixel
space, the decoder's in latent space. We build three VAE families with bilinear
layers in the encoder, the decoder, or both, and subject each to the full analysis.
The encoder study confirms that the method transfers and that its features are
causal. The decoder study is the core of the extension: the direct transfer fails,
the failure has an exact mathematical mechanism, and the corrected protocol turns a
3/10 generation success rate into 9/10. Along the way we prove a symmetry pathology
of purely bilinear decoders, delimit the method against gradient-based and PCA
baselines, and settle a natural alignment hypothesis with a controlled null result.

\section{Scope of reproducibility}
\label{sec:scope}

We organize the contributions of \citet{pearce2025bilinear} into seven claims. Each is
a single testable statement about a different property of bilinear MLPs, so the claims
can be verified or falsified independently. We use the labels C1--C7 throughout the
paper to link experiments to the claims they evaluate.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 --- Interpretability.} Eigendecomposing a trained bilinear MLP's
interaction matrices, using the weights alone and no input data, yields top
eigenvectors that correspond to human-recognizable visual structure, such as digit
strokes on MNIST and garment edges on Fashion-MNIST.

\item \textbf{C2 --- Low rank.} Each class's interaction matrix is effectively
low-rank: truncating the model to its few largest-magnitude eigenvectors preserves
classification accuracy, consistently across model sizes, and the retained
eigenvectors are consistent across training runs.

\item \textbf{C3 --- Regularization shapes features.} The character of the learned
eigenfeatures is controlled by regularization: Gaussian input noise sparsifies and
cleans them, revealing overfitting artifacts in unregularized models, while weight
decay lowers the effective rank; geometric augmentations reshape features in
predictable ways.

\item \textbf{C4 --- Causality.} Eigenvectors are causally meaningful rather than
merely descriptive: adversarial masks constructed purely from the weights, with no
gradients or per-input optimization, reduce accuracy and induce targeted
misclassification far more than matched random masks.

\item \textbf{C5 --- Circuits.} In a bilinear transformer, the interactions that
construct an SAE output feature from SAE input features can be read off the bilinear
tensor directly, recovering an interpretable circuit (the sentiment-negation circuit)
grounded in the weights rather than in gradient-based approximations.

\item \textbf{C6 --- Low-rank approximation of language features.} SAE output features
of bilinear transformers are well approximated by the top one or two eigenvectors of
their interaction matrices, increasingly so the longer the SAE is trained, extending
the low-rank finding of C2 from classifiers to language models.

\item \textbf{C7 --- Practicality.} Adopting bilinear MLPs costs little: they match
gated activations (ReGLU, SwiGLU) in compute-matched language model training, and an
existing SwiGLU transformer can be converted into a bilinear one by fine-tuning the
gate away at a modest loss penalty.
\end{itemize}

Claims C1--C4 concern image classifiers and correspond to Figures~2--7 and
Appendices~A--F and~M of the original paper. Claims C5--C7 concern language models and
correspond to Figures~8--9 and Appendices~G--J and~N--O.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Model descriptions}
\label{sec:models}

\paragraph{Bilinear layers and interaction matrices.}
For an input $\vx \in \R^d$, a bilinear layer computes
$g(\vx) = (\mW\vx) \odot (\mV\vx)$ with $\mW, \mV \in \R^{k \times d}$. A single
output $a$ of the layer is $g(\vx)_a = (\vw_{a}^\top \vx)(\vv_{a}^\top \vx) =
\vx^\top (\vw_a \vv_a^\top) \vx$, so the collection of outputs is described by a
third-order tensor with entries $b_{aij} = w_{ai} v_{aj}$. For any output direction
$\vu$, contracting the tensor along the output axis gives an interaction matrix
$\mQ = \vu \cdot_{\text{out}} \tB$, and since only the symmetric part of $\mQ$
contributes to $\vx^\top \mQ \vx$, we symmetrize
$\mQ \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{2}(\mQ + \mQ^\top)$. The spectral theorem then gives
\begin{equation}
  \vx^\top \mQ \vx = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i \, (\vv_i^\top \vx)^2,
  \label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vv_i$ and real eigenvalues $\lambda_i$: the layer's
output along $\vu$ is a weighted sum of squared projections onto orthogonal input
directions. All analyses in this paper are instances of Equation~\ref{eq:eig} for
different choices of $\vu$. One consequence deserves emphasis because it drives the
central finding of our extension: \textbf{$\mQ$ is linear in the probe direction
$\vu$}, so probes that overlap produce interaction matrices that overlap.

\paragraph{Image classifiers.}
Following the original architecture, the vision models consist of a linear embedding
from $784$ pixels to $d_{\text{hidden}}$ dimensions, a single bilinear layer, and a
linear classification head, with no biases. Unless stated otherwise we use
$d_{\text{hidden}} = 512$; the model-size experiments (C2) sweep
$d_{\text{hidden}} \in \{30, 50, 100, 300, 500, 1000\}$. Eigenvectors are computed in
the embedding space and projected back to pixel space through the embedding weights
for visualization.

\paragraph{Language models.}
The language experiments use the transformer checkpoints released by the original
authors: a 6-layer TinyStories model ($d_{\text{model}} = 512$, $d_{\text{hidden}} =
2048$, 8 heads, context 256, called \emph{ts-tiny} in the paper), and two FineWeb
models following GPT-2 small and medium shapes with 12 and 16 layers
(\emph{fw-small}, \emph{fw-medium}). In all of them the MLP block is bilinear, so the
interaction matrix for any output direction of a block is computable from the
down-projection and the two up-projections. SAE dictionaries with a TopK activation
\citep{gao2024scaling} trained by the original authors on MLP inputs, MLP outputs and
the mid-layer residual stream are likewise public, and we use them unchanged. For C7
we train 6-layer TinyStories transformers from scratch, identical except for the MLP
activation (bilinear, ReGLU, or SwiGLU), and fine-tune the pretrained
TinyLlama-1.1B \citep{zhang2024tinyllama}, whose SwiGLU activation
$x \cdot \sigma(\beta x)$ we anneal from $\beta = 1$ to $\beta = 0$, turning the
gated MLP into a bilinear one.

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

\paragraph{MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and KMNIST.} All three contain 70{,}000 grayscale
$28 \times 28$ images in 10 classes with the standard 60{,}000/10{,}000 split
\citep{lecun1998mnist, xiao2017fashion, clanuwat2018kmnist}. Images are normalized
to $[0, 1]$ and flattened. The reproduction uses the first two; the extension uses
all three, with KMNIST (ten Hiragana characters with high intra-class variability)
serving as the hardest generality check.

\paragraph{TinyStories.} The 6-layer transformer and its SAEs were trained by the
original authors on a cleaned, deduplicated version of TinyStories
\citep{eldan2023tinystories} with a custom lowercase 4096-token BPE tokenizer.
The cleaned dataset was never published. We therefore run all
TinyStories evaluations, and our own C7 training runs, on the public
\texttt{roneneldan/TinyStories} corpus, lowercased and tokenized with the authors'
released tokenizer. Section~\ref{sec:deviations} quantifies the consequences.

\paragraph{FineWeb.} For the FineWeb models we sample sequences from the public
\texttt{sample-10BT} configuration of FineWeb \citep{penedo2024fineweb}, tokenized to
length 512 with the Mixtral tokenizer, matching the models' training setup.

\subsection{Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:hyper}

All vision experiments use the training configuration reported in the original paper:
AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $1.0$, batch size 2048, cosine
annealing, 50--100 epochs, and additive Gaussian input noise with standard deviation
0.5 unless the experiment sweeps it. The C7 TinyStories runs use the original Table~2
setup (batch 512, context 256, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay 0.1, linear
decay, 5 epochs). Two deliberate deviations, both forced and both documented in
Section~\ref{sec:deviations}, are the fine-tuning learning rate for TinyLlama and the
evaluation dataset for TinyStories models. A complete listing of hyperparameters,
including the extension's VAE training setup, is given in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\subsection{Experimental setup and code}
\label{sec:setup}

Our experiments build on the authors' released library
(\url{https://github.com/tdooms/bilinear-decomposition}), which we vendor unmodified;
every experiment is a self-contained script in our repository that imports from it.
The auxiliary research repository referenced by the paper is no longer available on
GitHub, so all training, evaluation and figure code beyond the library was
re-implemented from the descriptions in the paper. Each experiment writes its figures
alongside a JSON file with its quantitative results, and expensive steps (model
training sweeps, eigendecompositions of up to 8192 SAE features) are cached to disk.

\textbf{C1} trains classifiers on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST and visualizes top
eigenvectors per class (Figures~2--3 of the original; our Figure~\ref{fig:c1}).
\textbf{C2} sweeps six hidden sizes over five seeds each, measures cross-seed cosine
similarity of eigenvectors by rank, and evaluates classifiers truncated to their
top-$k$ eigenvectors per class via Equation~\ref{eq:eig} (original Figure~5 and
Appendix~F). \textbf{C3} sweeps Gaussian input noise, weight decay, and geometric
augmentations, and inspects the resulting eigenvectors and their $(L_1/L_2)^2$
near-sparsity (original Figure~4 and Appendices~B,~E). \textbf{C4} builds adversarial
masks from rows of the pseudoinverse of the top-10 eigenvectors per class, adds them
to test images, and measures accuracy and targeted misclassification against matched
random masks (original Figure~7 and Appendix~M). \textbf{C5} contracts the bilinear
tensor of layer~4 of the TinyStories model with the encoder direction of the
``not-good'' SAE feature, transforms the interaction matrix into the input SAE basis,
and reproduces the interaction submatrix, the eigenvector projection geometry, and
the rank-2 activation approximation (original Figure~8 and Appendices~N,~O).
\textbf{C6} approximates SAE feature activations with truncated eigendecompositions
across the three transformers and across SAEs trained for increasing durations
(original Figure~9, Table~5, Appendix~G). \textbf{C7} trains the three
activation-function variants under identical conditions and logs wall-clock
timestamps with periodic validation, so that both the constant-epochs and the
constant-time comparison of the original Table~6 come from one set of runs, and
fine-tunes TinyLlama with linear $\beta$-annealing over the first 30\% of 100{,}000
steps of roughly 400M FineWeb tokens (original Appendix~J).

Auxiliary analyses of the original paper that are not required to evaluate the seven
claims (per-example explanations, the HOSVD analysis, the reverse-engineering
challenge of the original Figure~6, and the feature-example tables) are also
reproduced and available in the repository.

\subsection{Extension setup: bilinear layers in variational autoencoders}
\label{sec:extmethod}

The extension studies three VAE families on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and KMNIST, with
the bilinear layer placed so that exactly one question is asked at a time:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Encoder-bilinear}: $\vx(784) \to \text{Linear}(256) \to
  \text{Bilinear}(512) \to \vmu, \log\vsigma^2 \in \R^{10}$, with an ordinary MLP
  decoder. For any latent direction $\vmu^* \in \R^{10}$, the activation
  $\vmu^{*\top}\vmu(\vx)$ is a quadratic form with interaction matrix
  $\mQ \in \R^{784 \times 784}$; its eigenvectors are pixel-space patterns.
\item \textbf{Decoder-bilinear}: an ordinary MLP encoder, and
  $\vz(10) \to \text{Linear}(256) \to \text{Bilinear}(512) \to \text{Linear}(784)
  \to \text{sigmoid}$, all without biases. For any pixel-space target
  $\vp^* \in \R^{784}$, the pre-sigmoid output satisfies
  $\vp^{*\top}\text{logits}(\vz) = \vz^\top \mQ_{\text{dec}} \vz$ with
  $\mQ_{\text{dec}} \in \R^{d \times d}$; its eigenvectors are latent directions.
  The sigmoid is monotone per pixel and is excluded from the quadratic form.
\item \textbf{Fully bilinear}: both halves bilinear. Its first version fails for a
  reason we prove in Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}; the working version adds a linear
  skip connection in the decoder and KL annealing.
\end{itemize}

All VAEs are trained with the standard ELBO (binary cross-entropy reconstruction,
unit-Gaussian prior), AdamW, and light input noise, for 30 epochs with
best-validation checkpointing; each configuration is additionally trained with five
seeds where seed statistics are reported. Two causal protocols recur throughout.
On the encoder side, the \emph{maximally activating input test} scales the top
eigenvector of a class-mean direction to a realistic image norm, passes it through
the actual encoder, and asks whether it lands nearest its own class's mean latent;
its baseline is the same procedure on untrained networks, reported over ten seeded
initializations because single draws proved noisy (an untrained encoder's degenerate
class means make this baseline substantially above the naive $1/10$). On the
decoder side, the \emph{causal generation test} decodes the top eigenvector of
$\mQ_{\text{dec}}$ scaled to the mean latent norm, re-encodes the image, and asks
the same question. Every number reported below is written by the corresponding
experiment to a results file in the repository, which contains 50 extension
experiments in total; the main text presents the informative subset.

\subsection{Computational requirements}
\label{sec:compute}

All experiments ran on a shared university server with NVIDIA RTX A5500 GPUs (24~GB).
The vision reproduction is light: the largest pieces, the 30-model sweeps behind C2,
complete in about one GPU hour each. The language experiments dominate the
reproduction: regenerating the eigendecompositions and activations for C6 takes
roughly 4 GPU hours, the three C7 TinyStories runs about 5.3 GPU hours each, and the
two TinyLlama fine-tuning arms about 8 GPU hours each. The extension's models are
small ($\sim$1M parameters) and train in minutes; its roughly 60 training runs and
50 analysis batteries add an estimated 15 GPU hours. The full study amounts to
approximately 65 GPU hours, corresponding to an estimated 4.5~kgCO$_2$eq
\citep{lacoste2019quantifying}.

\subsection{C1: top eigenvectors are recognizable visual features}

We train one classifier per dataset with the original configuration and decompose
each class's interaction matrix. Figure~\ref{fig:c1} shows the top positive
eigenvector for classes 1--5 of MNIST and Fashion-MNIST alongside the eigenvalue
spectrum and top eigenvectors for digit~5. The reproduced eigenfeatures match the
original paper's closely: MNIST eigenvectors capture stroke segments specific to each
digit, while Fashion-MNIST eigenvectors act as localized edge detectors, for example
the leg gap of trousers. Because the activation of an eigenvector is quadratic,
$\lambda (\vv^\top \vx)^2$, its sign is arbitrary, and the red and blue regions
implement the XOR-like behavior described in the original: overlap with either region
alone activates the feature, overlap with both cancels. Per-digit spectra for all ten
MNIST classes are reproduced in the repository and match the original Appendix~A.

\subsection{C2: the computation is effectively low-rank and stable across runs}

We train five seeds at each of six hidden sizes and compare eigenvectors across
seeds. The top positive eigenvector reaches a mean cross-seed cosine similarity
between 0.73 and 0.88 depending on model size, declining with eigenvector rank, and
larger models remain similar deeper into the spectrum (rank-15 similarity 0.13 for
size 30 versus 0.48 for size 1000). Both the levels and the ordering match the
original Figure~5A. One protocol detail differs: we exclude the diagonal when
averaging over seed pairs, since comparing a run with itself contributes a constant
similarity of one; the original figure's slightly higher values are consistent with
this difference.

Truncating each classifier to its top-$k$ eigenvectors per class via
Equation~\ref{eq:eig} preserves accuracy with remarkably few components: at $k = 15$
the accuracy drop ranges from 0.01\% (size 30) to 1.4\% (size 1000), and by $k = 30$
all sizes are within noise of their full accuracy (94.8\% to 98.5\% across sizes).
This matches the original Figure~5B and Appendix~F. Figure~\ref{fig:c2} shows both
panels.

\subsection{C3: regularization controls the character of the features}

Sweeping the training-time Gaussian input noise from 0 to 0.8 reproduces the
qualitative transition of the original Figure~4: without noise the top eigenvector of
an unregularized model concentrates on scattered outlying pixels, a visible signature
of overfitting, and increasing noise produces progressively cleaner, more digit-like
features at essentially unchanged test accuracy (97.2\%--98.1\% across the sweep;
Figure~\ref{fig:c3}). The augmentation ablations of the original Appendix~B
reproduce as well: translation splits features into copies at multiple positions,
rotation broadens them, and blur smooths them, while none of the geometric
augmentations alone removes the edge-pixel overfitting that input noise removes. The
sparsity grid of the original Appendix~E confirms the quantitative half of the claim:
$(L_1/L_2)^2$ near-sparsity of the top-5 eigenvectors is governed almost entirely by
input noise, while the analogous statistic of the eigenvalue spectrum, the effective
rank, is reduced by weight decay.

\subsection{C4: weight-derived masks causally steer the model}

We reproduce the pseudoinverse-mask construction of the original Figure~7: for each
class, the rows of the pseudoinverse of its top-10 eigenvectors act as keys that
activate specific eigenvectors, and adding a scaled key to every test image steers
the model toward the corresponding class. Evaluated on the full test set, a mask of
standard deviation 0.3 built for a noise-regularized model (input noise 0.15) drops
accuracy from 97.9\% to 58.7\% while a permuted version of the same mask leaves it at
95.6\%, and raises targeted misclassification to 28.7\% against 0.6\% for the random
baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:c4}). For an unregularized model the second variant of the
experiment restricts masks to border pixels active in under 1\% of samples, purely
exploiting the overfitting structure visible in C3, and still drops accuracy from
97.6\% to 23.5\% (random baseline 40.5\%).

The original Appendix~M studies how mask effectiveness varies with regularization,
and our quantitative version confirms its message: as training noise increases from 0
to 0.3, the same attack at mask strength 0.3 recovers from 31.8\% to 74.4\% accuracy,
with random-mask baselines between 85.9\% and 97.2\% throughout, so weight-derived
masks remain far more effective than chance even against the most regularized model.

\subsection{C5: the sentiment-negation circuit is recoverable from the weights}

Using the public 6-layer TinyStories model and its layer-4 SAEs, we contract the
bilinear tensor with the encoder direction of the ``not-good'' output feature
(index 1882) and transform the interaction matrix into the input SAE feature basis.
The reproduced interaction submatrix (Figure~\ref{fig:c5}, left) shows the AND-gate
structure of the original Figure~8A: negative-sentiment input features interact
strongly and positively with negation features while both groups have negligible
self-interactions, and the one positive-sentiment feature interacts with the opposite
sign. Projecting the input features onto the top positive and negative eigenvectors
of the interaction matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:c5}, right) reproduces the circuit
geometry of Figure~8B, including the positions of the ``bad''$-$``good'' unembedding
difference and the residual-stream activation of an isolated ``not'' token. The two
outlier eigenvalues of the interaction matrix reproduce exactly at the paper's
reported precision, $+0.62$ and $-0.66$, as expected for quantities computed from
the weights alone. The rank-2 approximation of the feature's activation via
Equation~\ref{eq:eig} concentrates along the diagonal at large activations, as in

\subsection{C7: bilinear layers as a drop-in replacement}

We train three 6-layer TinyStories transformers from scratch, identical except for
the MLP activation, and derive both rows of the original Table~6 from one set of
runs: the constant-epochs comparison from the final validation loss, and the

trails SwiGLU by only 0.006 nats (1.237 against 1.231), the same order as the
paper's 0.016 gap, and is indistinguishable from ReGLU; at constant time the
bilinear model sits between the two gated variants, 0.005 nats behind SwiGLU. The
full constant-time equalization of the original Table~6 did not manifest, for a
mechanical reason: in our implementation the three variants run at nearly identical
wall-clock speed (the gate is a negligible element-wise operation), so constant
time buys the slower variants almost no extra steps. The original's own footnote
anticipates this, noting that the constant-time result depends on implementation
efficiency. Because our runs use the public TinyStories corpus rather than the
authors' unavailable cleaned version, absolute losses are not comparable to the
original; the claim concerns the differences between activation functions, which we
measure under identical conditions.

For the fine-tuning half of the claim we anneal TinyLlama-1.1B's gate away over the
first 30\% of a 100{,}000-step run on roughly 400M FineWeb tokens
(Figure~\ref{fig:c7}). The loss trajectory reproduces the shape of the original

plausibly account for the larger residual: our stability-driven lower learning rate
slows recovery, and our compute budget matches the paper's token count, which its
authors already describe as insufficient for full convergence. The qualitative
conclusion, that a pretrained gated transformer can be converted to a bilinear one at
a modest and shrinking penalty, stands.

\section{Results: extension to variational autoencoders}
\label{sec:extension}

This section reports the extension outlined in Section~\ref{sec:extmethod}. We
present the encoder study first (Section~\ref{sec:enc}), then the decoder study that
forms the core of the extension (Sections~\ref{sec:dec}--\ref{sec:rank}), a symmetry
result for purely bilinear decoders (Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}), the comparison
against gradient-based and PCA baselines that delimits what bilinearity buys
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), and a controlled null result on encoder--decoder
alignment (Section~\ref{sec:alignment}). Section~\ref{sec:extlimits} collects the
limitations.

\subsection{The encoder: weight-based features transfer and are causal}
\label{sec:enc}

Applying Equation~\ref{eq:eig} to the bilinear encoder with class-mean latent
directions yields pixel-space eigenvectors that behave like their classifier
counterparts in every qualitative respect: they are visually structured, stable
across training runs (rank-1 cross-seed cosine $0.81 \pm 0.06$, inside the
0.80--0.90 range the original reports for classifiers), and organized around visual
similarity. The 10-by-10 matrix of cosine similarities between top eigenvectors
reproduces human confusability with no label ever seen during training: the
(4,\,9) pair reaches 0.81, (0,\,6) 0.61, (1,\,7) 0.57, while the visually dissimilar
(3,\,8) pair stays at 0.21. One quantitative property does change: whereas the
original's classifiers are captured by roughly three eigenvectors per class,
matching the true encoder activation to correlation $r > 0.9$ here requires rank 8
to 13, and up to 20 for the most complex digit. An encoder trained to represent a
full data distribution stores more directions per concept than one trained to make
a single decision, a point we return to in Section~\ref{sec:rank}.

The central encoder result is causal. In the maximally activating input test
(Figure~\ref{fig:extmaxact}), the synthetic image built from a class's top
eigenvector, with no data and no gradients, is correctly recognized by the encoder
for \textbf{9 of 10} MNIST classes, \textbf{10 of 10} on Fashion-MNIST, and
\textbf{9 of 10} on KMNIST. The corresponding random-initialization baselines,
measured over ten seeded untrained networks, are $4.4 \pm 1.6$, $3.5 \pm 1.9$, and
$3.5 \pm 1.6$; the trained scores exceed every individual baseline draw. The single
MNIST failure (digit 8 encodes nearest to 1) and the single KMNIST failure both fall
on the class each dataset's own similarity matrix marks as most entangled. A second,
independent causal probe adapts the original paper's pseudoinverse masks to the
encoder: adding a weight-derived key of scale $\sigma = 1$ to arbitrary test images
steers their encodings into a chosen target class 67.7\% of the time, against 3.5\%
for permuted masks, rising to 94.8\% at $\sigma = 4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:extmaxact},
right). The pseudoinverse construction matters: simply adding the scaled eigenvector
itself steers \emph{below} chance, reproducing on the encoder the same effect that
motivated pseudoinverse keys in the original work.

\subsection{The decoder: a failure with a mechanism, and its correction}
\label{sec:dec}

The decoder invites the mirrored analysis: choose a pixel-space target
$\vp^*$, eigendecompose $\mQ_{\text{dec}}$, and decode the top eigenvector to see
what the weights generate for that target. The natural choice of target,
the class-mean image, fails badly. The top eigenvectors of all ten classes are
nearly the same latent direction (mean pairwise cosine 0.842 on MNIST, 0.910 on
Fashion-MNIST, 0.833 on KMNIST), every class decodes to nearly the same blob
(the middle row of Figure~\ref{fig:extsynth}), causal generation succeeds for only
3, 2, and 1 of 10 classes on the three datasets, and synthesis pays a 5.8-fold
mean-squared-error penalty over the same model's actual reconstructions. A control
with untrained networks shows the shared direction is already present at
initialization (cosine 0.646 on MNIST) and is amplified by training, so it is
neither purely learned nor purely architectural.

The mechanism is elementary once seen. $\mQ$ is linear in the probe
(Section~\ref{sec:models}), and the ten class-mean images overlap heavily: their
mean pairwise cosine is 0.742 on MNIST, 0.771 on Fashion-MNIST, and 0.821 on
KMNIST, because all classes share the central mass of ink. Overlapping probes
therefore produce overlapping interaction matrices, and the ``universal generative
direction'' is largely a property of the questions asked, not of the decoder.
Maximizing an all-positive uncentered target also trivially rewards ink wherever
ink is common, which is exactly the class-agnostic blob the synthesis produces.

The correction is to center the targets, $\vp^*_c - \bar{\vp}$ with $\bar{\vp}$ the
mean of the class means, asking what distinguishes class $c$ from the average image.
Everything changes (Table~\ref{tab:centering} and the bottom row of
Figure~\ref{fig:extsynth}): the cross-class eigenvector similarity drops to
0.40--0.46 against untrained-network baselines of 0.27--0.30, causal generation
rises to \textbf{9/10} (MNIST), \textbf{6/10} (Fashion-MNIST) and \textbf{7/10}
(KMNIST), the synthesis penalty falls to 2.5--5.1$\times$, and the decoded
eigenvectors are recognizable, class-specific images. On the fully bilinear model,
where seed statistics are available across six checkpoints, centering lifts causal
generation from $4.7 \pm 0.7$ to $\mathbf{9.5 \pm 0.5}$ of 10.

Centering is not merely a trick that happens to work; it is the canonical
correction. Because $\mQ$ is linear in the probe,
\begin{equation}
  \mQ(\vp - \bar{\vp}) \;=\; \mQ(\vp) - \mQ(\bar{\vp}),
\end{equation}
verified numerically to $9 \times 10^{-5}$: centering subtracts the \emph{entire}
shared interaction matrix, not an approximation of it. We tested three alternatives
under identical conditions. Pairwise-contrast targets ($\vp^*_c$ minus the nearest
confusable class mean) reach 7/10 with slightly lower eigenvector overlap but worse
synthesis error. Deflation, which removes the top eigenvector of the shared matrix
$\mQ(\bar{\vp})$ rather than the whole matrix, makes things \emph{worse} than raw
targets (cross-class overlap 0.890, causal 3/10), because $\mQ(\bar{\vp})$ is far
from rank one: its leading component carries only 39\% of its spectral mass, and a
deflation-depth sweep degrades monotonically. A generalized eigenproblem against a
positive-definite surrogate of the shared matrix reaches only 4/10. Finally,
one-vs-rest contrast targets are algebraically identical to centered targets up to
the scalar $10/9$, so that family collapses to centering. The lesson generalizes
beyond VAEs: any application of weight-based eigendecomposition on the output side
of a bilinear network, wherever natural probe directions overlap, should center its
probes.

\subsection{The corrected structure is rank one, robustly}
\label{sec:rank}

How much of the decoder's generative behavior does the top eigenvector carry?
Truncation experiments answer: essentially all of it. Reconstructing a class from
the top $k$ eigenvectors of its centered interaction matrix, the single top
eigenvector already attains the full centered result (synthesis MSE
$0.028 \pm 0.005$ across six checkpoints; causal $9.5 \pm 0.5$), the curves are flat
in $k$ beyond one, and under a magnitude ordering additional eigenvectors actively
hurt. Together with the encoder's rank of 8--13 (Section~\ref{sec:enc}) this yields
a clean asymmetry we did not anticipate: in these models, \emph{recognition is
high-rank while generation is rank one per class}. Full decoding of the class-mean
latent remains about four times more accurate than any weight-only synthesis, so the
quadratic form is a faithful rank-one summary of what the decoder does per class,
not a replacement for the decoder.

Because the latent dimension in these experiments (10) coincides with the number of
classes, we tested whether the rank-one structure and the centering correction are
artifacts of a cramped latent space, retraining the decoder-bilinear model at
$d \in \{10, 20, 32\}$ with three seeds each (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}). They are not:
centered causal generation reaches an exact $9.0 \pm 0.0$ at both $d = 20$ and
$d = 32$, the single top eigenvector remains optimal even with sixteen positive
eigenvalues available, the leading eigenvalue's share of the positive spectral mass
is nearly constant (0.69) across dimensions, and since the untrained baseline falls
with dimension while the centered overlap does not, the learned class structure
becomes more distinguishable from chance as the latent space widens.

to genuinely shared visual components. Where classes truly overlap, no choice of
target direction can pull their generative directions apart.

\subsection{A symmetry pathology of purely bilinear decoders}
\label{sec:symmetry}

The first version of the fully bilinear VAE trains poorly (reconstruction MSE
0.064, more than twice the working version). The reason is structural.

\begin{proposition}
A decoder composed of bias-free linear maps and bilinear layers, followed by any
element-wise output function, satisfies $\mathrm{decode}(\vz) =
\mathrm{decode}(-\vz)$ for all $\vz$.
\end{proposition}

Every hidden unit of the bilinear layer is a product of two linear functionals of
$\vz$, hence an even function; bias-free linear maps preserve evenness, and an
element-wise output function applied to an even pre-activation remains even. We
verified the identity numerically to exactly zero. The consequence is that the
latent space collapses onto a quotient in which $\vz$ and $-\vz$ are
indistinguishable, halving the usable code space; the same identity holds for the
decoder-bilinear model of Section~\ref{sec:dec}, where the flexible MLP encoder
learns around it, but a bilinear encoder cannot. A single linear skip connection
from $\vz$ to the decoder output breaks the symmetry and recovers normal training
(MSE 0.028); this repaired version is the fully bilinear model used everywhere
above. Figure~\ref{fig:extsym} shows the identity and the fix side by side. The
practical rule is short: purely bilinear decoders need a linear term.

\subsection{What does bilinearity buy? Gradient and PCA baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

A fair skeptic asks whether the results of Section~\ref{sec:dec} need a bilinear
decoder at all, since gradient machinery can search for latent directions on any
decoder. We ran the comparison under identical conditions (centered targets, the
same norm budget, the same causal test) on an ordinary MLP-decoder VAE: gradient
ascent on the target objective (Adam, 300 steps, 8 restarts) and, as a cheap
variant, the single-backward-pass Jacobian direction $\mJ^\top \vp^*$ at $\vz = 0$.
Table~\ref{tab:jacobian} shows the outcome. Gradient methods match and slightly
exceed the weight-based protocol: both reach 10/10 causal generation on all four
vanilla-VAE training seeds, with synthesis error as good or better, and gradient
ascent applied to the bilinear decoder itself also reaches 10/10. The bilinear
constraint therefore buys no raw synthesis capability. What it buys is the form of
the answer: the eigendecomposition is computed once from the weights with no data
and no gradients, is exact over the entire latent space rather than a local optimum
or a linearization at one point, and returns the complete spectrum of generative
and suppressive directions with their strengths in a single object. The
one-step Jacobian makes the contrast concrete: on the bias-free bilinear decoder it
is structurally degenerate, since $\mJ^\top \vp^* \equiv 0$ at $\vz = 0$ (a
quadratic has no linear term at the origin), while the weight-based
eigendecomposition is exactly the second-order object that takes its place.

A second baseline tests whether the weight-based directions are simply the
principal components of the latent code, as PCA-based methods for generative models
would extract them \citep{harkonen2020ganspace}. They are not. Measured against a
chance floor of random orthonormal bases, which is high in a 10-dimensional space,
the top one and top three PCA directions of the posterior match the centered
eigenvectors no better than chance at any sample size from 10 to 5000 (for example
0.22 against a floor of $0.26 \pm 0.09$ for the top direction), and only the
complete 10-direction basis edges marginally above its floor (0.70 against
$0.61 \pm 0.04$). PCA captures the directions along which the data varies; the
interaction matrix captures the directions with which the decoder writes a chosen
pattern. The two are complementary, and no amount of sampling makes one reproduce
the other.

\subsection{Encoder--decoder alignment: a controlled null result}
\label{sec:alignment}

In the fully bilinear model it is natural to hypothesize that the two analyses meet
in the middle: that the latent direction the encoder uses to represent a class
aligns with the latent direction the decoder uses to generate it. It does not. The
measured cosine between the two, averaged over classes, is $0.026 \pm 0.129$ across
six trained models, against an empirical random baseline of $0.258 \pm 0.056$;
per-class values swing between $-0.74$ and $+0.52$ and flip sign across seeds.

We pushed the hypothesis as far as it can go by training the model \emph{for}
alignment: an auxiliary loss that maximizes the cosine between the encoder Jacobian
$\partial \vmu_k / \partial \vx$ and the decoder Jacobian
$\partial \, \mathrm{decode}(\vz) / \partial z_k$. Two findings resulted
(Figure~\ref{fig:extalign}). First, the initial implementation of this loss
computed both Jacobians outside the computation graph and therefore contributed
zero gradient; the checkpoint trained with it is indistinguishable from an
unregularized one. We mention this because silently detached auxiliary losses are
an easy failure mode to miss, and because it means the loss had never actually been
tested. Second, after repairing the loss and training fresh seeds at two strengths,
the strong setting demonstrably optimizes its own objective, raising the mean
Jacobian cosine from 0.376 to 0.465, and the eigenvector alignment metric still
does not move: $-0.061 \pm 0.116$, inside the random band. The same null holds on
Fashion-MNIST once seed spread is accounted for (six checkpoints average
$0.045 \pm 0.233$; a single checkpoint had suggested otherwise). Encoder and
decoder eigenstructures are genuinely independent objects in these models, and a
training signal that aligns local Jacobians does not align them.

\subsection{Limitations of the extension}
\label{sec:extlimits}

Four boundaries should be stated plainly. First, weight-based synthesis never
matches actual decoding: even after centering, its error is 2.5 to 5 times the
model's reconstruction error, consistent with the rank-one reading of
Section~\ref{sec:rank}. Second, bilinear VAEs are not more disentangled than
ordinary ones; on a mutual-information-gap proxy every bilinear variant scores
below the vanilla baseline (0.06--0.10 against 0.13). The one latent-quality metric
where a bilinear variant wins is class structure: the decoder-bilinear model
attains the best linear-probe accuracy (0.908 against the vanilla 0.890) and
silhouette score of the four architectures. Third, the eigenvector \emph{basis} on
the decoder side is seed-stable only at its top: pixel-space syntheses agree across
seeds at cosine 0.99, but the underlying latent eigenvectors beyond the first agree
only at about 0.2, so claims should attach to the top direction, not the basis.
Fourth, everything here is at the scale of $28 \times 28$ grayscale images and
latent dimensions up to 32; a convolutional variant on CIFAR-10 trained but
produced a latent space with near-zero class structure (linear probe 0.36), so we
make no claim beyond the small-image regime.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the reproduction supports}

The central methodological claim of \citet{pearce2025bilinear} holds up well under
independent reproduction: for bilinear MLPs, the weights alone support analyses that
normally require data, and the structures they reveal are real in the strongest
available sense, since masks derived purely from eigenvectors steer the model (C4)
and eigenvector approximations track SAE feature activations on held-out text (C6).
We found the vision results robust to full retraining from scratch, including their
quantitative details, and the language results reproducible from the released
checkpoints and dictionaries.

\subsection{What the extension establishes}

The extension's findings compress into five statements. Weight-based features
survive the loss of labels and are causal on the encoder side, at 9--10 of 10
across three datasets against properly measured baselines. On the output side the
method fails by default and the failure is a lesson with reach beyond VAEs: the
interaction matrix is linear in the probe, so overlapping probes, which are the
natural choice almost everywhere, manufacture spurious shared structure, and
centering the probes is the exact correction. The corrected picture is a clean
asymmetry, high-rank recognition against rank-one generation, that is stable
across latent dimensions and datasets. Purely bilinear decoders carry an exact
sign symmetry that must be broken by a linear term. And two disciplined negatives
delimit the method honestly: gradient baselines match its raw capability, locating
its value in closed-form, data-free, globally valid structure rather than
synthesis power, and the tempting hypothesis that encoder and decoder
eigenstructures align is false even under direct optimization pressure.

\subsection{Deviations and their causes}
\label{sec:deviations}

We record four deviations in the reproduction, each traced to its cause.

\paragraph{The cleaned TinyStories dataset is unavailable.} The published
TinyStories checkpoint reaches a per-token loss of about 2.9 on the public
TinyStories validation set, against the 1.34 the paper reports on the authors'
cleaned corpus. We ruled out our evaluation pipeline as the cause by testing
tokenization and punctuation normalization, story packing and padding conventions,
attention masking, and rotary embedding variants; none moves the number
appreciably, and the same evaluation applied to our own from-scratch models
produces losses in the expected range (Table~\ref{tab:c7}). The gap therefore
reflects a genuinely different training distribution that can no longer be
obtained, since the research repository that produced it is no longer public. Most
results are unaffected, but one is: the loss-added metric of the original
Appendix~G evaluates the model slightly off its training distribution, where an SAE
reconstruction can act as a denoiser, compressing and occasionally inverting the
metric's absolute values. The ordering between hook points survives; the absolute
curve does not.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning diverges at the reported learning rate.} The original
Appendix~J describes fine-tuning TinyLlama with the FineWeb configuration of the
paper's Table~3, whose learning rate of $6 \times 10^{-4}$ is a from-scratch
pretraining value. At our batch size of 4096 tokens per step, both the fine-tuning
arm and the continued-training baseline diverge immediately after warmup at that
rate, from loss 2.7 to above 7. At $3 \times 10^{-5}$ both arms train stably. We
report results at the stable rate and flag the discrepancy.

\paragraph{Protocol details.} Two smaller items: our cross-seed similarity curves
(C2) exclude self-pairs from the average, which lowers the curves slightly relative
to including them; and in C6 the ordering of fw-small and fw-medium is swapped
relative to the original, within the reported range.

\paragraph{Software drift.} The vendored library predates breaking changes in its
dependencies: current \texttt{transformers} drops non-registered buffers when
loading the released checkpoints, requiring the attention mask and rotary caches to
be rebuilt after loading, and current PyTorch rejects the training-path attention
call, which passes an explicit mask together with \texttt{is\_causal}. Our scripts
carry small, documented shims for both. Neither affects results; both would stop a
naive rerun.

\subsection{What was easy and what was difficult}

The vision experiments were straightforward: the library's decomposition code is
clean, models train in minutes, and the paper describes the setups precisely enough
to re-implement every figure. The language experiments were harder for reasons that
have little to do with the method. The missing research repository meant all
experiment code had to be reconstructed from prose; the missing cleaned dataset put
absolute loss comparisons permanently out of reach; and locating the correct public
artifacts required some detective work, for instance the paper's ``ts-tiny'' model
being published under the name \texttt{ts-medium}. In the extension, the recurring
difficulty was of a different kind: almost every headline number changed once its
baseline was measured properly. Single-draw random baselines understated encoder
causal baselines by half, a PCA comparison looked meaningful until a chance floor
was computed, a correlation between spectral mass and reconstruction error
dissolved under rank statistics and leave-one-out checks, and an auxiliary loss
turned out to be silently detached from the gradient. The corrected versions of all
of these are what we report, and we would summarize both halves of the project with
the same advice: in this kind of analysis, the baseline is the experiment.

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}

This work evaluates and extends an interpretability method; it introduces no new
model capabilities. The adversarial masks of C4 and the encoder steering of the
extension are constructed from full knowledge of a model's weights and target small
MNIST-scale models, so they do not constitute a practical attack surface beyond
what gradient-based attacks already provide in the white-box setting. We expect the
net impact of better weight-based transparency tools to be positive.
